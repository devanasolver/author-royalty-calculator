# The Letter

The rain had stopped by morning. Anas opened the window and looked out over the city, thinking about everything that had changed since the letter arrived. Somewhere below, a moped coughed awake, and the bakery on the corner was already perfuming the whole street with the smell of the first batch.

He made coffee the way his grandfather had taught him, slowly, with more patience than the drink deserved. The letter sat on the table where he had left it, folded once, creased twice, read four times.

*Some news waits for no one*, his grandfather used to say. Anas turned the cup in his hands and decided that today, finally, he would answer.

The walk to the post office took him past the bookshop with the blue door. He had spent whole summers in its back room, trading chores for secondhand novels and advice he did not know he needed.

Madame Rkia was behind the counter, as always. She looked up over her glasses and smiled the way people smile when they already know your news before you do.

He remembered the first time he had seen the sea, years ago, and how small it had made every worry feel. The city did that too, in its own crowded, indifferent, affectionate way.

A cat followed him for half a block, then lost interest at the fishmonger's corner. He chose to take it as a sign, though of what he could not have said.

The afternoon stretched long and golden. Somewhere a radio played an old song, and the whole neighborhood seemed to hum along without quite knowing the words.

He bought bread, oranges, and a small bunch of mint, because some habits are worth keeping no matter what the postman brings or takes away.

At the cafe, Si Driss slid a glass of tea across the counter before he even sat down. **'You look like a man who made a decision,'** he said. 'Those are the dangerous ones.'

***

By noon the reply was written, sealed, and sent. He stood on the steps of the post office feeling lighter than he had in months, as if the city itself had agreed to start over.

Night came gently. He read the letter one more time, then put it in the drawer where he kept the things that mattered, and slept better than he had in weeks.
